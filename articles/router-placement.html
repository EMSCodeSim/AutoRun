Where should you put a Wi-Fi router?

A room-by-room guide to improving wireless coverage before spending money on new hardware.

Published October 9, 2026 · Prepared with AI editorial assistance · Original explanatory illustration; not a product test or photograph.

Start with the actual problem

A router's position can make a noticeable difference to coverage, but it cannot fix every slow connection. Before moving equipment, check whether the problem affects every device or only a particular room. Test the same device near the router and again where performance is poor. If the speed and reliability improve nearby, wireless coverage is worth investigating.

Also check whether the internet provider has reported an outage. A device can be connected to Wi-Fi while its internet connection is unavailable. Fixing Wi-Fi placement will not repair a provider outage.

Choose a central, open location

For many homes, a reasonably central, elevated position gives the router more opportunity to reach rooms in different directions. Placing it behind a metal cabinet, inside a closet or close to large obstructions can interfere with the path between the router and devices.

You do not have to put the router in the geometric center of the house at any cost. Consider where people actually need connectivity, where the incoming connection is located, electrical safety and whether its antennas can be positioned according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Know what walls and floors change

Walls, floors and other building materials can attenuate wireless signals. The impact depends on the material, thickness, frequency band and layout. A signal that crosses multiple dense barriers may be less reliable than one with a clearer path. If you use equipment with multiple bands, experiment with the manufacturer-recommended settings before making broad changes.

A simple way to compare locations is to use the same device, stand in the same test spots and repeat comparable tasks after each change. Keep notes on room, position and whether the problem improved. That is more informative than relying on a single test result.

Compare three low-cost improvements

First, move the router out of an enclosure and away from large metal objects if practical. Second, position it where it is less obstructed by furniture and high-traffic household equipment. Third, if your home is larger or has difficult construction, consider wired access points or a properly configured mesh system only after confirming a coverage gap.

Avoid buying a new router simply because a speed-test score is disappointing. The limiting factor might be your internet plan, local device, an overcrowded channel or the destination service.

When placement is not enough

If the device is still slow near the router, investigate internet service, hardware condition and network configuration. If the router is far from a room and moving it is impractical, a wired Ethernet connection may be the most predictable option for fixed devices.

Never factory reset network equipment as a first troubleshooting step. Resetting can erase passwords, network settings and provider-specific configuration. Follow official instructions, and keep a record of symptoms before contacting support.

Quick decision checklist

Test two devices and two locations. Check for a provider outage. Move the router out of enclosed or obstructed locations. Repeat comparable tests. Only consider buying additional networking hardware if you have evidence of a coverage problem.
