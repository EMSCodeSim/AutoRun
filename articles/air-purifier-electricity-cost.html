How Much Electricity Does an Air Purifier Use? Calculate the Cost of Running It All Day

Calculate 24/7 air purifier electricity costs from watts and your rate, then compare room size, filtration performance and replacement-filter expenses.

Published 2026-11-27 · Prepared with automated editorial assistance · Illustration is an original explanatory diagram, not a product photo or test result.

Calculate electricity use from watts and hours

Electricity use is measured in kilowatt-hours, not simply watts. Divide the purifier's power in watts by 1,000, multiply by hours of operation, and then multiply the resulting kWh by your electricity rate in dollars per kWh. For example, a hypothetical 40-watt purifier running for 24 hours uses 0.96 kWh. At an illustrative $0.20/kWh, that is $0.192 per day, $5.76 for 30 days and $70.08 for 365 days. These are mathematical examples, not tested power measurements or local utility prices.

Find the power rating on the product label or manufacturer specifications. A maximum wattage is not necessarily the power consumed in sleep or automatic mode. Your utility bill may include fixed charges and taxes that are not captured by the simple energy calculation. To avoid overstating savings, distinguish the variable energy charge from the total household bill.

Compare eight hours with continuous operation

At a constant 40 watts, running eight hours uses 0.32 kWh daily while running 24 hours uses 0.96 kWh. At the illustrative $0.20/kWh rate, the 30-day energy charges are $1.92 and $5.76 respectively. This comparison isolates runtime and assumes the same fan setting. A purifier on automatic mode may change its draw throughout the day, so an actual monthly bill can differ.

Less runtime also means less time filtering the room. EPA says longer operating time and higher fan speeds generally increase the amount of air filtered. During wildfire smoke or other significant particle exposure, filtration effectiveness and public-health guidance may matter more than modest energy savings. Portable filtration supplements source control and ventilation with clean outdoor air when appropriate; it does not replace either.

Account for fan speed and automatic mode

Purifiers often have low, high, sleep and sensor-controlled settings. Higher speeds generally deliver more filtered air but may draw more electricity and make more noise. To estimate a mixed schedule, calculate each operating period separately. A hypothetical unit using 20 watts for 16 hours and 50 watts for eight hours consumes 0.32 plus 0.40, or 0.72 kWh daily. At $0.20/kWh, the illustrative 30-day energy charge is $4.32.

An automatic setting can vary power consumption according to sensed particles, and a single advertised wattage cannot reveal its actual duty cycle. Do not assume that the quietest setting provides the same particle removal as the highest setting. Check manufacturer documentation for operating power and clean-air delivery at the speed you plan to use. If those values are unavailable, treat a calculation based on maximum wattage as a rough scenario rather than a measurement.

Choose adequate clean-air delivery for the room

Clean Air Delivery Rate, or CADR, measures how quickly an air cleaner delivers air filtered of specified particles. EPA's consumer sizing guidance suggests a minimum CADR of approximately 130 cubic feet per minute for a 200-square-foot room, and 195 cfm for a 300-square-foot room, assuming an eight-foot ceiling. These are approximate sizing guides, not guarantees of a particular health result. Higher ceilings and open floor plans may require more capacity.

Measure length times width to estimate square footage, then compare the purifier's particle CADR and the manufacturer's recommended room size. Smoke or fine-particle CADR is particularly useful when the concern is small particles. A low-wattage purifier that is undersized for the room may not be a useful purchase. Conversely, an unnecessarily large unit can cost more to purchase and may draw more power than the space requires.

Interpret ENERGY STAR efficiency claims carefully

ENERGY STAR reports that a standard room air cleaner running continuously consumes about 394 kWh annually, and that certified room air cleaners are more than 50 percent more energy-efficient than standard models. Those are program-level estimates rather than predictions for every model. The actual cost of a specific purifier depends on its wattage, speed, runtime and the rate charged by your utility. Compare products with similar cleaning capacity rather than selecting the lowest wattage in isolation.

The current ENERGY STAR room-air-cleaner specification became effective October 9, 2025. It uses an Integrated Energy Factor that relates particle clean-air delivery to electrical input and accounts for active and standby operation. Greater clean-air delivery per watt can indicate better energy efficiency. It does not measure removal of every odor or gas, and an ENERGY STAR label is not a medical endorsement of a particular product.

Measure your own appliance for a closer estimate

A compatible plug-in electricity meter can record the kWh used by a household plug-in purifier over several representative days. Follow the meter's ratings and safety instructions, use an undamaged outlet and cord, and do not defeat appliance safety features. Measure for long enough to capture night settings and automatic changes in fan speed. A measurement made during an unusually smoky period may not represent typical operation throughout the year.

Divide measured kWh by the number of days to find average daily use. Multiply that figure by 30 and your own electricity rate to estimate a 30-day energy charge. Write down the dates, settings and conditions so the result can be interpreted later. If you cannot measure directly, a manufacturer power specification can support a useful estimate, but do not present the result as hands-on testing or promise exact bill savings.

Include filter replacement and proper placement

The full cost of ownership includes replacement filters, not just electricity. EPA notes that filters need maintenance or replacement and that dirty, overloaded filters can perform poorly. Before purchasing, identify the exact filter part number, replacement guidance and availability. Compare annual filter spending alongside estimated annual energy use. A cheaper-to-run purifier may still be expensive to maintain if it requires costly proprietary filters.

Keep air intakes and outlets unobstructed and follow the manufacturer's placement instructions. ENERGY STAR advises keeping curtains and other objects away from the airflow path. Do not tuck the purifier behind furniture merely to hide it. Particle CADR is not a standardized measure of gas removal; EPA notes that activated carbon and other specialized media may be used for certain gases, but their effectiveness depends on the design and amount of material.

Avoid common air-cleaner misconceptions

A portable purifier does not eliminate all pollutants, repair moisture damage, stop a mold source or make unsafe air automatically safe. EPA identifies source reduction and ventilation with clean outdoor air as fundamental indoor-air strategies, with filtration serving as a supplement. If indoor air is affected by combustion, persistent moisture or strong chemical emissions, investigate the underlying cause instead of simply operating a purifier at maximum speed.

Avoid ozone generators in occupied rooms. EPA warns that ozone is a lung irritant and says devices intentionally generating it should not be used around people. Some electronic air-cleaning technologies may also emit ozone. Evaluate device claims carefully and prioritize appropriately sized particle filtration, reliable product information and routine maintenance over vague promises that a machine will sterilize all indoor air.

Make a decision using both cost and cleaning performance

Begin with the actual room size and your filtration goal. Choose a suitable CADR, check the noise level at a usable fan speed, and look up that speed's wattage. Calculate estimated monthly kWh and energy charges using your own rate. Add the annual cost of filters and other consumables, and check whether the unit can operate safely in the intended location. Keep these assumptions visible so the comparison remains fair.

If you already own a functioning purifier, first inspect its filter and measure or estimate actual consumption before replacing it solely for electricity savings. A more efficient model can be worthwhile when the existing one is undersized, loud, unreliable or costly to maintain, but do not assume energy savings alone will repay the purchase. The best practical choice is a purifier that provides enough filtered air in the room at an acceptable total operating cost.
