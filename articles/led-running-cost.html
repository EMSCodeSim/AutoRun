How much does an LED light bulb cost to run?

Use wattage and hours to compare lighting energy costs without confusing brightness and power.

Published 2026-10-27 · Prepared with automated editorial assistance · Illustration is an original explanatory diagram, not a product photo or test result.

Start with the actual wattage

Brightness and electricity use are not the same measure. The watt number describes the bulb's power use under specified conditions, while lumens describe the amount of visible light emitted. For comparing how much lighting costs to operate, the watt rating and hours of use matter most.

Read the specific lamp packaging or documentation instead of assuming all bulbs marketed as the same brightness consume the same number of watts.

A useful calculation

A hypothetical 9-watt lamp running five hours a day for 30 days consumes 1.35 kWh: 9 ÷ 1,000 × 5 × 30. At an example price of $0.15 per kWh, the energy portion of the monthly cost is about $0.20. Actual rates, run times and power use will differ.

A full household electricity bill often includes fixed fees and other charges; this example covers the bulb's estimated energy consumption alone.

Comparing two options fairly

If two light sources provide comparable useful illumination, comparing their electrical wattages is a reasonable starting point for energy costs. But wattage is not the only purchase consideration: brightness, beam direction, fit, color appearance, dimmer compatibility and expected lifespan matter too.

An inexpensive bulb that flickers with a particular dimmer or creates uncomfortable glare may be a poor purchase even if its running cost is low. Check compatibility and manufacturer recommendations.

How long the light is on matters

A light that runs for ten minutes a day has much less opportunity to affect energy costs than a light used for long evening hours. Estimate typical hours rather than multiplying every lamp by 24 hours.

For shared spaces or irregular schedules, take a representative week and note how often each light is used. That can make your assumptions more realistic.

Safety and compatibility

Use bulbs suited to the fixture's electrical and thermal ratings. Enclosed fixtures, outdoor locations and dimmers may have specific lamp requirements. Do not exceed the fixture's rated limits or modify electrical wiring without appropriate expertise.

Try the calculation yourself

Use the site's electricity cost calculator with your bulb's watts and your bill's rate. Then compare the estimated energy savings with the price of replacing a working bulb. This lets you decide based on your situation rather than an exaggerated savings claim.
