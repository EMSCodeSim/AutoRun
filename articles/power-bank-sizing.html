Charging · Explainer

How large a power bank do you really need?

Understand watt-hours, capacity claims, losses and travel considerations before choosing portable charging.

Published 2026-10-20 · Prepared with automated editorial assistance · Illustration is an original explanatory diagram, not a product photo or test result.

Start with your actual use case

A small battery pack that tops up a phone during commuting serves a different purpose from a large pack intended to keep multiple devices operating through a trip. Consider which devices you will charge, whether they need USB-C Power Delivery and how often you will have access to an outlet.

Do not choose solely by the biggest milliamp-hour number on the package. That single number does not describe output voltage, conversion losses or whether a device can negotiate the power it needs.

Understand mAh and Wh

Milliamp-hours (mAh) describe electric charge capacity at a stated voltage. Watt-hours (Wh) represent stored energy. To estimate watt-hours from a specified nominal voltage, multiply amp-hours by volts. Because devices use different nominal voltages, two mAh figures cannot always be compared directly.

For illustration, a 10,000 mAh pack rated at a nominal 3.7 volts corresponds to about 37 Wh of nominal stored energy (10 Ah × 3.7 V). This is not a claim about usable USB output energy.

Why you will not receive all the stated energy

Voltage conversion, heat, cable and connector losses, internal reserve, device charging circuitry and environmental conditions affect what reaches the connected device. For that reason, advertised capacity is not equivalent to the exact amount that can be delivered to your phone.

A useful comparison is the manufacturer's declared Wh rating alongside supported USB output ratings, physical size, quality and warranty. Real delivered capacity must be measured under specified conditions.

Check output capability separately

A battery pack's total capacity and maximum output power answer different questions. Capacity relates to how long it may supply energy; output rating relates to whether it can power or charge a particular device at the desired rate. Check the laptop or phone's requirements, the pack's port ratings and the cable's capability together.

Multiple devices plugged into one pack may share a total output budget. Look for manufacturer documentation that explains simultaneous charging limits rather than assuming every port reaches its advertised individual maximum.

Air travel and safety

Airline and aviation rules apply to lithium batteries and power banks, and restrictions depend on their watt-hour ratings and other conditions. Consult the FAA PackSafe rules and your airline before traveling. Do not rely on a generic social-media chart as your sole source.

Stop using a battery pack that is visibly swollen, leaking, damaged or abnormally hot. Keep it away from excessive heat and follow the manufacturer's handling instructions.

An efficient purchase decision

Write down device power needs, desired number of recharges, size/weight preference and travel restrictions. Compare verified Wh and output specs, then decide whether the extra capacity is worth carrying. More capacity is not always a better fit.
