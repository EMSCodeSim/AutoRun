kW vs. kWh: what your electricity bill actually measures

Understand power and energy, see the math, and estimate costs with a realistic example.

Published 2026-10-13 · Prepared with automated editorial assistance · Illustration is an original explanatory diagram, not a product photo or test result.

The short answer

A kilowatt (kW) describes a rate of power use. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) measures how much energy is used over time. Utility bills commonly include charges based on kilowatt-hours consumed. The distinction matters because two appliances with the same power rating can use very different amounts of energy depending on how long they run.

Convert watts into kilowatts

Power is often stated in watts. Divide a watt value by 1,000 to express the same power in kilowatts. A 100-watt load is 0.1 kW. A 1,500-watt load is 1.5 kW. The rating on an appliance may describe a maximum or nominal draw, not its continuous measured average.

If a device cycles on and off, such as a refrigerator or heater, simple nameplate estimates may overstate or understate actual consumption. A compatible energy meter or manufacturer-provided consumption data can make the estimate more useful.

Convert power and time into energy

Energy in kilowatt-hours is power in kilowatts multiplied by running time in hours. A hypothetical steady 100-watt device run for eight hours would use 0.8 kWh. Over 30 identical days, that would total 24 kWh.

A device run at twice the power for half the time can consume the same energy if all other factors are equal. That is why wattage alone cannot tell you what a device will cost over a month.

Estimate the part of the bill related to energy

Multiply estimated kWh by the relevant electricity rate in dollars per kWh. At an example rate of $0.15/kWh, 24 kWh would represent $3.60 in energy charges. Actual bills can also include fixed charges, seasonal rates, taxes, time-of-use pricing, demand charges or other utility-specific adjustments.

The site's calculator estimates energy cost, not your full utility bill. Use the exact rate and billing period from your utility when possible; avoid treating an arbitrary national average as your household price.

Why actual use can differ

Heating elements, compressors, electronic power supplies and other systems may change their electricity draw during operation. Standby modes can consume energy, and operating conditions matter. For a more accurate estimate, measure average consumption over a representative period instead of assuming the device draws its maximum wattage constantly.
