How Much Electricity Does a Dehumidifier Use? Calculate the Real Cost

Estimate dehumidifier running costs from watts, hours and your electric rate, then use capacity and efficiency ratings to choose wisely.

Published 2026-11-13 · Prepared with automated editorial assistance · Illustration is an original explanatory diagram, not a product photo or test result.

The short answer: watts, hours and electricity rate

A dehumidifier uses electricity to move air and, for a typical refrigerant-based portable unit, operate a refrigeration system that condenses moisture. Its energy cost depends on the power it draws, how long it actually runs and your utility's price per kilowatt-hour (kWh). There is no single reliable monthly dollar amount for every dehumidifier: room moisture, temperature, settings and equipment efficiency all matter.

Use this formula for a first estimate: watts ÷ 1,000 × operating hours × dollars per kWh. If you know the appliance's rated watts but not its actual average draw, treat the answer as a scenario rather than a measured bill. A humidistat may stop and restart the compressor, so hours plugged in are not necessarily hours at full power.

Worked example: a 400-watt unit

Suppose a hypothetical dehumidifier draws 400 watts while actively operating. If it draws that power for eight hours in one day, its energy use is 0.4 kW × 8 hours = 3.2 kWh. At an illustrative electricity rate of $0.18 per kWh, the energy charge is $0.576, or about $0.58 for that day. Repeat the same use for 30 days and the calculation gives 96 kWh and $17.28 in energy charges.

Now compare a much heavier-use scenario: the same 400-watt draw for 16 hours per day over 30 days equals 192 kWh and $34.56 at that same illustrative rate. Neither example is a forecast for your home, and neither includes fixed utility fees or rate tiers. The important lesson is that operating time can change the answer as much as the equipment's wattage.

Find your own electricity rate before estimating

Look for the per-kWh energy charge on your utility bill, including any applicable variable delivery charges if you want to estimate the marginal cost of extra electricity use. Some bills have time-of-use periods, seasonal pricing, tiered rates or credits, so a single rate can be an approximation. Avoid substituting a national average when your actual bill is available.

If your bill shows total dollars divided by total kWh, that blended figure may include fixed fees that would not increase when the dehumidifier runs. For an incremental-cost estimate, use the charges that actually vary with consumption. If pricing changes by hour, calculate each time block separately and add the results.

Measure use instead of assuming nonstop operation

A plug-in energy meter rated for the appliance's electrical load can record accumulated kWh over several days. Follow the meter and dehumidifier instructions, use a suitable grounded outlet and never exceed the meter's rating. A week that includes damp and dry days is more informative than a short measurement during a single compressor cycle.

Record the meter's starting and ending kWh readings and the dates. Divide the difference by the number of days to find average daily energy use; multiply by your relevant rate for an estimated daily cost. Repeat during a wetter period if you want a more representative seasonal range. Do not treat the compressor's nameplate wattage as a measured daily average.

Why humidity, temperature and settings change consumption

A damp basement, laundry area or room with an ongoing moisture source may require more moisture removal than a relatively dry room. A humidistat can reduce runtime once its target is reached, while a lower target may keep the equipment working longer. Actual behavior depends on the model and conditions; track indoor relative humidity rather than adjusting settings blindly.

Temperature also matters. ENERGY STAR notes that conventional refrigerant dehumidifiers can experience coil frosting when the surrounding air is below about 65°F, and that lower-temperature-capable or automatic defrost models may be appropriate. If a unit repeatedly frosts, follow its instructions instead of assuming continuous operation is effective moisture removal.

Capacity in pints per day is not the same as electricity use

Dehumidifier capacity, often listed as pints per day, describes water removal under specified test conditions. It is not a power rating and cannot be converted directly into a monthly electricity bill. A higher-capacity unit may remove more water in a shorter time, but the right choice depends on how damp and large the space is.

Be careful when comparing an older machine's advertised capacity with a new model. ENERGY STAR explains that test-condition changes beginning around 2020 led to lower-looking capacity ratings on some otherwise comparable portable units. Compare units using current, consistent test methods and the manufacturer's specifications, not just the biggest printed pint number.

Read the efficiency rating: liters per kWh

ENERGY STAR explains dehumidifier efficiency using integrated energy factor (IEF), expressed in liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour. Higher IEF generally means more moisture removed per unit of energy under standardized test conditions. That makes it more useful for comparing efficiency than comparing wattage alone, especially when capacities differ.

ENERGY STAR's current dehumidifier criteria took effect October 1, 2025. Its product listings and criteria can help you compare certified models by capacity and efficiency. A certification or lab rating is not a promise that every household will achieve the same runtime or savings; actual room conditions and use patterns still matter.

Lower operating cost by addressing the moisture source

Before purchasing a larger machine, look for obvious sources of unwanted water: plumbing leaks, seepage, blocked drainage or wet materials. A dehumidifier can remove moisture from air, but it is not a substitute for fixing a leak or persistent water intrusion. Investigate unexplained dampness and use qualified help for structural, electrical or significant water-damage problems.

Keep the unit's intake and exhaust clear according to its manual, clean filters as directed and maintain its bucket or drain system. Place it on an appropriate stable surface with the manufacturer's required clearance. A clogged filter or poorly arranged drain can undermine performance even if the device is still consuming electricity.

When buying a replacement makes sense

If you already own a functioning dehumidifier, estimate its measured annual kWh before deciding that a more efficient model will pay for itself. For example, an illustrative 100-kWh annual reduction at $0.18/kWh saves $18 in variable energy charges per year. Compare that with the purchase cost, expected service life, capacity, drainage features, noise and warranty; do not claim a guaranteed payback from an efficiency label alone.

For a new purchase, first identify the room's approximate size and moisture conditions, then use ENERGY STAR's sizing guidance and certified-product information. Confirm the model is suitable for the operating temperature and has a convenient bucket or continuous-drain option. Buy for the moisture problem you actually have rather than choosing the largest unit by default.

Quick checklist before you decide

Check your bill for a useful per-kWh rate. Find the dehumidifier's wattage or measure kWh with a properly rated meter. Record realistic active operating hours and calculate a range rather than one exact monthly figure. Check whether the room has a leak or drainage problem, and monitor humidity and frost behavior.

If replacing equipment, compare current capacity ratings and integrated energy factor, not just marketing claims. Use Practical Pick's electricity-cost calculator to try multiple wattages, runtimes and rates. A calculation informed by your own measurements is more valuable than a generic promise of savings.
